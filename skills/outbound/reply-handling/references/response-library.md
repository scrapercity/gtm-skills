# Response Library

Original responses for each reply class. Every draft is sendable as written: no brackets, no blanks to fill. The only variables are merge fields with a fallback, `{{first_name|there}}` and `{{company|your team}}`, which render safely even when empty. Dates and times in the drafts are examples; swap them for open slots on the rep's calendar or the time on the booked invite.

Three rules sit above every draft:

1. Use only facts in the user's brief: their offer, prices, results and integrations as the user stated them. If the brief has no proof point, the reply goes out without one. Never write a bracket for someone to fill later, because a rushed rep either sends it broken or invents a claim to fill it.
2. Two to three sentences (Berman's follow-up guide), never over 75 words, one ask at most, and the reply ends when the job is done. "Not interested" and "not now" replies end without a question.
3. Sign with the sender's first name in a thread the prospect started. A new thread to someone who never saw the first email (R11) signs with full name, title and company, and goes out from the sequence so it carries the same footer and opt-out line as every other touch.

Principles from Alex Berman's follow-up guide and templates page: answer interest within 5 minutes, keep "send info" replies to a 2 to 3 sentence summary with a proposed call, don't send pricing, proposals or PDFs by email, acknowledge and remove "not interested", log "later" dates.

## 1. Interested: meeting

**R1. Two slots**

> Great, thanks {{first_name|there}}. Does Tuesday at 10:30am or Wednesday at 2pm Central work for 15 minutes? I'll send the invite to this address.

**R2. Booking link** (only when the brief includes the link; it goes on the line under the draft. No link in the brief: use R1)

> Thanks {{first_name|there}}. Grab any open slot on my calendar below. If none of them fit, send me two times that do and I'll make one work.

**R3. They accepted or proposed a time**

> Great, Thursday, October 15 at 2:00pm Central works. The invite is on its way to this address; forward it to anyone else who should join.

Restate the day, the date and the time, say the invite is on its way, and invite them to forward it. No question, no agenda. Send the calendar invite in the same 5 minutes: Berman's follow-up guide says to send it immediately after they agree.

Time zone: use the zone the prospect wrote. If they named a time with no zone ("2pm works"), take it from their signature, the business address on the lead record, or the phone area code, create the invite in that zone and name the zone in the draft. Only when none of those shows it does the draft say "2:00pm your time" and ask, in the same line, which zone they are in; the invite goes out with the answer. Never assume the sender's zone.

Times you propose (R1, R4, R6) fall outside the prospect's busy hours. Restaurants: Toast lists peak hours of 11am to 2pm and 5pm to 8pm for quick service and 5pm to 10pm for casual dining (Toast, June 2026), so offer slots between 2:30pm and 4:30pm their time. Other industries: outside the business's posted opening rush, or the hours the user gives.

**R4. Interested, but busy this week**

> No rush. Would next Monday at 10am or Tuesday at 3pm Central suit you better?

## 2. Interested: info first

**R5. "Send more info"** (Berman: short summary, a proposed call, no PDF)

> Happy to. What's useful depends on how {{company|your team}} handles this today, so 15 minutes on a call will get you more than a deck. Would Tuesday at 10am or Wednesday at 2pm Central work?

If the brief has a one-line offer and a measured client result, put them before the call ask, word for word from the brief. If it has neither, send R5 as written.

**R6. "How much does it cost?"** (hot: answer inside 5 minutes)

Berman's templates page gives the shape: "Pricing depends on [Variable 1] and [Variable 2], so it varies", then "I can walk you through the different options in 15 minutes." His follow-up guide adds that pricing discussions over email rarely lead to deals. So the first sentence answers what the price depends on, using the variables in the brief; if the brief names none, the one size measure the offer plainly scales with (people on payroll, locations, seats), and Open Items asks the user to confirm it. No number goes in the email.

> Fair question. It depends mainly on how many locations {{company|your team}} runs and how often you need it, so it varies. I can walk you through the options in 15 minutes: does Wednesday at 3pm or Thursday at 10am Central work?

**R7. "Is there a case study?"**

> Good question. I'd rather walk you through how it would work for {{company|your team}} than send something generic. Does Wednesday at 2pm Central work for 15 minutes?

If the brief names a client result, answer yes and give it in one sentence: client type, before, after, time taken. Never describe a case study the brief doesn't contain.

## 3. Interested: product question

**R8. Integration or fit question the brief answers**

Answer yes or no in the first four words, using the brief's own wording, then ask for the call. If the brief doesn't cover the question, send R8b and route the question to the solutions engineer.

**R8b. Holding reply** (the brief doesn't answer it)

> Good question, and I want to get it right. I'm checking with our product lead and will reply today with a straight answer.

**R9. The answer is no**

> Not today, no. If that's a must-have, better you hear it now than on a call. If it isn't, I can show you what it does do in 15 minutes. Thursday at 11am Central?

## 4. Referral

**R10. Thank the referrer (named person)**

> Thanks {{first_name|there}}, that helps. I'll reach out and mention you sent me.

(No question. This reply closes the thread with the referrer.)

**R11. First email to the referred person** (a new thread, sent from the referral sequence)

The referred person never saw the first email, so R11 has to stand on its own. A note that says "Tom said to talk to you, see below" makes them read someone else's thread to find out why they should care. Build it in this order:

| Part | Rule |
|-|-|
| Subject | Names the referrer: "{{referrer_first_name}} suggested I reach out" or "{{referrer_first_name}} pointed me to you". Under 50 characters (Berman's subject limit). |
| Line 1 | Why them: the referrer's name plus the job the referrer said they own ("Tom said you run payroll for both locations"). One sentence. Use "suggested" only if the referrer named this person. |
| Line 2 | One reason to care, written for their industry and their job, from the brief: the problem the offer removes for someone in that seat. |
| Line 3 | One proof from the brief: a client result, the price, or a fact that removes a likely worry. Skip it if the brief has none. |
| Line 4 | One ask with two times in their time zone, or the booking link. |

Never paste the original email below it, quote the referrer's words back at length, or call it a "follow-up". Under 75 words without the subject (Berman's cold email limit).

Illustrative example (fictional brief: outsourced bookkeeping for independent vet clinics, $450 a month per clinic, books closed by the 10th of each month):

> Subject: Tom suggested I reach out
>
> Hi Dana, Tom said you handle the books for both clinics. When bookkeeping waits until the last appointment is done, the month closes late. We close clinic books by the 10th of each month for a flat $450 per clinic. Would Tuesday at 10am or Wednesday at 2pm Mountain work for 15 minutes?

Sequence follow-ups to the referred person get one new fact each (a second proof point, a question about how they close the month today), as Berman's follow-up guide asks of every follow-up.

**R12. Role only ("our marketing team handles that")**

> Thanks {{first_name|there}}. Who on that team should I talk to?

## 5. Objections

**R13. No need**

> Fair enough, thanks for the quick reply. I'll close this out and won't follow up.

(Treat it as not interested. No feedback question.)

**R14. Already use a competitor and happy with it**

> Makes sense, and if it's working I won't try to talk you out of it. If month-end close ever starts eating someone's week, reply here and I'll show you how we handle it.

Acknowledge, don't argue, and leave one reason to come back: the hardest part of the job in the prospect's industry that the offer takes over (for restaurant payroll, tips and tip credits across shifts and locations; for a dental review tool, patients who agree at checkout and forget). Name the problem as a condition ("if X ever starts eating someone's week"), never as a claim about the competitor or about what "most" buyers find. Berman's follow-up guide calls a competitor reply good news: they are in the market. The contact goes to nurture with one useful touch every 4 to 6 weeks (Berman's nurture cadence), first touch 6 weeks after the reply, so the CRM row carries an exact date.

**R15. Price or no budget**

> Understood, thanks for being straight with me. I'll check back at the start of the new year and won't email before then.

If the brief offers a pilot or a smaller starting plan, offer it in one sentence instead of the date, and end with "Want to try that first?"

**R16. "We tried something like this and it didn't work"**

> That's useful to know, thanks. What went wrong last time? If it's something we do differently, I'll tell you how. If it isn't, I'll say so.

**R17. "Just send me something to read"**

> Sure. What's the one question you'd want it to answer? I'll write back with that instead of a deck.

**R18. "Is this automated?" or "Is this AI?"**

> Fair question. I use public business data to decide who to contact, and I read every reply myself. If you want to know exactly where your details came from, ask and I'll tell you.

**R27. "We built it internally" or "we do it in-house"**

> Makes sense. If it's running fine, I'll leave it there. If upkeep starts eating someone's week, reply here and I'll show you what we'd replace and what we wouldn't.

For a service the buyer does by hand (payroll, bookkeeping, scheduling), name the part the brief says you can take off one person's plate, so keeping the in-house person and buying from you are not either-or. Example from a fictional bookkeeping brief: "Good, then someone already knows your numbers. Some clinics keep that person on bills and hand us the month-end close. If yours has it covered, I'll leave it there."

**R34. "Too expensive"** (they have seen or heard the price)

> Fair. If the cost is close to what your team spends on this now, it isn't worth switching, and I'd rather you know that up front. If it isn't close, 15 minutes Thursday at 11am Central would show you where the difference comes from.

Use a number from the brief to size the trade only when it is the brief's own figure ("the Boise clinic was spending 6 hours a week before"). Never quote a competitor's price, a salary or a tool cost the brief doesn't give.

**R35. "Our current system already does that"** (a bundled feature: their POS, practice software or bank already offers it)

> Makes sense. With ours, a person on our side does the work, so nobody on your team has to run it. If your system already handles it without anyone's time, you're set.

Replace "the work" with the specific job from the brief (running payroll, filing quarterly taxes, chasing reviews). Describe only what the user's offer does. Never say the bundled tool can't do something unless the brief says so.

### Ready lines for the objections that come up most

| Objection | The line does | The line never does |
|-|-|-|
| No need, not interested (R13, R25) | Accepts and stops | Asks for feedback |
| Happy with a competitor (R14) | Acknowledges, names one industry problem the offer takes over, nurture date | Knocks the competitor or claims what it lacks |
| Too expensive (R34) | Admits it may not pay, offers a 15 minute comparison | Argues value, invents ROI |
| No budget (R15) | Takes a date or offers the brief's pilot | Pushes for a meeting now |
| Our system already does it (R35) | Separates "the feature exists" from "someone does the work" | Claims the bundled tool falls short |
| We do it in-house (R27) | Offers to take one part off one person's plate | Implies the in-house person does it badly |
| Send me info (R5, R17) | Asks the one question the info should answer | Sends a deck |
| Evaluating options (R28) | Asks the decision date, offers time with the group | Asks for the competitor list |

When the user's brief or request names an objection not in this table, write a ready line for it in the same shape: acknowledge in under 10 words, one brief-backed fact, an easy out or one ask.

**R28. "We're evaluating other options" or "we're in procurement"**

> Good timing, then. When's the decision due? If it helps, I can take 20 minutes with whoever's weighing in, Tuesday at 2pm or Wednesday at 11am Central.

(If procurement owns the process, ask for the vendor form and the decision date, and log both in the CRM.)

## 6. Not now and timing

**R19. Specific future date ("try us next quarter")**

> Thanks {{first_name|there}}. I'll follow up the week of January 11 and won't email before then.

Confirm the date you'll write back, then stop. No question.

**R20. Vague "not now"**

> No problem. I'll check back in early March, and if that's still bad timing, just say so.

**R21. Re-engagement on the scheduled date** (new email, same thread)

> Hi {{first_name|there}}, you asked me to check back around now. Is this a better time, or should I close the file?

## 7. Wrong person and left company

**R22. Wrong person**

> Thanks {{first_name|there}}, sorry for the misfire. Who at {{company|your company}} handles this?

**R23. Left the company (human reply)**

> Thanks for letting me know, and good luck with what's next. Who took over your role at {{company|the company}}?

## 8. Not interested, unsubscribe and hostile

**R25. Not interested**

> Understood, thanks for letting me know. I won't follow up.

One or two lines, no question mark. A feedback question after "I won't follow up" breaks the promise in the same message. Learn why from the weekly class counts instead.

**R24. Unsubscribe** (removal line; polite opt-outs only)

Suppress the address in every tool the same day. Then pick one and write the same thing everywhere it appears (triage table, SLA table, CRM note): "No reply" or "Removal line (R24)". Never write "no sales reply" and then attach a draft; a reader can't tell whether something goes out.

> Sorry for the bother. You're removed and won't hear from me again.

**R26. Hostile opt-out or "how did you get my email"** (removal line, sent by a person)

> Sorry for the unwanted email. I found your address in a business contact database, and I've removed it so you won't hear from us again.

A person sends it, never automation, because a template that misreads a furious reply gets reported. Suppress first, then flag the reply for the weekly review. Don't argue or send a second message. The triage table says "Removal line (R26), sent by a person".

**Threat or data-request wording** ("my lawyer", "attorney", "cease and desist", "delete my data", "what data do you hold")

Threat wording: no reply from sales. Suppress, and forward the same day to the escalation owner the user names; that person decides, and the rep sends nothing. The triage table names the role, for example "No reply; forwarded to the sales manager". Data-request wording is handled like a complaint: suppress, then R26 or nothing, sent by a person.

## 9. Automatic replies

No response to OOO, auto-acknowledgements, ticket systems or bounces. Pause or suppress per `references/reply-taxonomy-and-routing.md`.

## 10. Response rules

| Rule | Why |
|-|-|
| Sendable as written; the only variables are merge fields with a fallback | A bracket left in a sent reply looks broken, and a rushed rep fills it with a guess |
| Facts only from the brief | A made-up result or integration in a reply surfaces on the first call |
| No proof in the brief, no proof in the reply | The reply still works with the call ask alone |
| Two to three sentences, never over 75 words | Berman: a 2 to 3 sentence summary; in B2B shorter often wins |
| One ask at most | Two questions get one answered or neither |
| Replies to a no, a later date or an unsubscribe end without a question | They told you the answer; a question reads as not listening |
| Two specific times with a time zone when booking | Berman's meeting ask names the slots ("Tuesday at 2pm or Thursday morning") |
| No PDF, deck, proposal or price sheet | Berman's guidance: push for the call |
| Reply in the same thread | Keeps context for both sides |
| No sequence touches while a conversation is open | A scheduled follow-up arriving mid-conversation looks automated |
| Always offer to remove on any hostile reply | A hostile reader who sees no way out reports the email as spam |

## 12. Meeting confirmations and no-shows

Used with the `cold-calling` no-show runbook. Ryan Reisert's sequence (GTMnow, November 2021): phone about a week out, email 12 to 24 hours before, phone 2 to 3 hours before if the email got no reply. A no-show goes back to the Priority bucket.

**R29. Booking confirmation** (within 5 minutes, with the invite; same shape as R3)

> Thanks {{first_name|there}}. You're set for Thursday, October 15 at 11:00am Central, and the invite is on its way; forward it to anyone else who should join.

**R30. Week-of check** (phone first; this email if no answer)

> Hi {{first_name|there}}, checking that Thursday at 11am Central still works for our call. If something came up, reply with a better time this week.

**R31. 24 hour reminder** (reply in the invite thread)

> Looking forward to tomorrow at 11am Central. If anything's changed on your side, reply here. The link is in the invite.

**R32. 2 to 3 hour call** (phone script, only when R31 got no reply)

> Hi {{first_name|there}}, calling to confirm our call at 11 today. Still good for you?

**R33. No-show** (once the start time has passed, after one call)

> Hi {{first_name|there}}, looks like today got away from you. No problem. Would Monday at 10am or Tuesday at 2pm Central work instead?

A no-show goes back to the Priority bucket until a new time is booked (Reisert).
